What this does — and doesn't — claim

Grounded is not true. A cited figure is as true as its document. It is labelled grounded, never proven.

Narrow on purpose. Figures, rules, simple claims and sums. Where no check can say yes, there is no answer.

One sealed run. One model, problems made by code. It says nothing about messy documents.

What it is, in one paragraph

A small AI model reads documents, writes rules as formulas and claims as comparisons, and works out answers. Four checks, one for each kind of work: a figure it cites must be in the document, a formula must pass its rule's tests, a claim is decided by a solver, a step is checked by exact arithmetic. Every facet is drawn in the material of what it stands on, and no answer is called truer than its weakest check.

Start here

The words this page uses. A facet is one piece of an answer on the glass: a figure, a rule, a claim, or one step of a sum. A facet bonds when its check passes; one that cannot is refused, and the reason is kept. Proven: worked out by code in exact fractions, with no rounding: nothing to take on trust. Grounded: a figure copied from a document, as true as the document. Proven against its tests: a formula that passed every test of its rule. Proven within its encoding: a claim decided exactly, as the model wrote it down. A claim's bar is the result it had to reach, written down before the sealed run. The sealed run is the measurement: problems the build was never tuned on, every file that could change a result frozen first. The lattice is the whole checked process (copy, write, decide, work out), set against one unchecked reply: the same model asked once, with nothing checking it. A harness is code that attacks a check with forged replies; a second reader is a separate piece of code, written apart from the check, that must rule the same way on every reply; a planted leak is a flaw put into a copy of a check on purpose, to show the harness finds it.

How it works

The reach of the whole engine is exactly the reach of its checks. So each kind of truth gets its own check, and every facet of the crystal carries the kind of check that made it and how much that check proves. There are four, and the model does the work for each; code only checks it.

  1. Cite. When a question needs a figure from a document, the model is shown the document and asked to copy the one line that states it. Its copy bonds only if it is in the document letter for letter, begins and ends at the edge of a word or number, sits in a line that is about what was asked, and holds that line's figure, whole. The value is then read from the document by code. The model never supplies a number.
  2. Refuse. If the document does not state the figure, states it two ways, or does not write it plainly, nothing bonds and the reason is recorded. That is the engine saying it cannot find it in your documents. No answer is given. The model is never asked whether a figure is there: the check decides that, from the document. Why: on 44 figures that were in their documents (dev problems, before the seal), the same model was asked both ways. Offered the word "none", it said none of 14 of them, and its copy bonded for 27. Asked only to copy, its copy bonded for 44.
  3. Write a rule. When a question gives a rule in words ("the unit price times the order size, plus the delivery charge"), the model writes it as a formula over a letter for each quantity: p × c + d. The formula is run on the rule's tests, chosen so that every near-miss of the rule (a sign changed, a side left out, a letter swapped) fails one. If it passes them all, the figures go into its letters and that is the calculation. It is proven against its tests: a formula built to pass exactly those tests and nothing else would pass too, and the attack below shows one doing it.
  4. Write a claim. When a question asks whether something is certain ("for 10 to 14 crates, is it certain the order comes to at least 300 pounds?"), the model writes the claim as a comparison: p × c + d ≥ 300. A small exact solver decides it from what is given: yes, no, or it depends. It is proven within its encoding: the verdict is exact about the claim as the model wrote it down, and the model may have written it down wrongly. The page counts how often it did.
  5. Work out. With the figures cited and the calculation known, it is grown as The Crystal grows one: one small step at a time, each reply checked by exact arithmetic. A step the model gets wrong is split into smaller steps it is asked again; a step that is already as small as steps go, and still wrong, is dropped (it dissolves), and the problem gets no answer rather than a wrong one. It starts from 419 steps already checked, from sjgant80-hub/the-crystal data/runs/sealed.json at 71274e4.
  6. Label. Every facet carries how true it is. The law is one line: a facet is only as strong as the weakest bond it stands on. A sum worked out exactly from a cited figure is grounded. A sum worked out from a tested formula is proven against its tests. No two strengths are ever counted or drawn as the same thing.

In the published sealed run: of the answers, 79 are grounded, 12 proven against their tests, 12 proven within their encoding and 12 proven; 0 were labelled stronger than what they stand on. The crystal holds 425 grounded bonds, 59 proven against tests, 12 proven within an encoding and 26 proven.

What the checked answers cost, against one unchecked reply

584 model calls for 144 problems (4.1 each), 1938 s of model time: 280 to cite figures, 24 to write rules, 24 to write claims and 256 to work out steps. One unchecked reply to each of the same problems, read generously, got 34 of 144 right; on the 24 problems that have no answer it gave a number 22 times. The lattice brought 139 of 144 out as they should come out.

The unchecked reply's instruction is the stronger of two tried on the 60 dev problems before the seal: told it may reply "cannot tell", the model got 16 of 60 right; not told, 15 of 60. With room for 192 tokens, 4 of its 60 replies ran to the limit.

What this does not prove
  • Grounded is not true. A cited figure is in the document, where the document says it is. If the document is wrong, the answer is wrong, and it is still grounded. In the published sealed run, 12 problems had a document that misstates a figure: 11 were answered, 11 with the document's figure, and 11 of those answers are not the true one. All 11 are labelled grounded. The label is the protection: it is never called proven.
  • "About what was asked" is a match of words. The check looks for the key words of the question in the line that is quoted. A question put in other words than the document's is beyond it, and such a figure is refused, not guessed.
  • A figure must be written plainly. Digits, with commas in the thousands and a decimal part, and nothing touching them that could change what they say. A figure with a sign in front, a leading point, a range, a unit run on to it, or a word like "million" after it is not read: the line cannot be cited from, and the question gets no answer. That is strict on purpose. Reading 5 where the document says 5 million would be worse.
  • The documents here are made by code. Short agreements, one statement to a line. Nothing here measures the check on long, messy documents.
  • Passing the tests is not being right. A formula is checked by running it on its rule's tests. The tests are chosen so that every near-miss of the rule fails one, but a formula built to pass exactly those tests and differ everywhere else passes too: the attack makes one for every rule and shows it bonding. That is why such a facet is labelled proven against its tests, never proven.
  • A claim is decided as it was written down. The solver's verdict is exact about the comparison the model wrote. If the model wrote "at most" for "at least", the verdict is exact about the wrong claim. Nothing here can tell, and the page counts how often the model's claim gave a verdict other than the question's own. Such a verdict is labelled proven within its encoding, never proven.
  • A division the model gets wrong is not split smaller. The arithmetic is grown as The Crystal grows it, as its seal froze it, and The Crystal has smaller steps for adding, taking away and multiplying, but none for dividing. When the model divides wrongly (it once wrote 26 ÷ 13 = 1.923), that step dissolves and the problem has no answer. So does a step it gets wrong that is already as small as steps go (in the sealed run it wrote 4270 + 8 = 4288). Such a problem is never given a wrong answer.
  • The solver is small. It decides one kind of claim completely: a sum of products and quotients, each quantity used once, each known exactly or between two figures. A claim that uses a quantity twice, or divides by something that could be nought, is refused, not guessed. A general solver would decide far more.
  • The tests stand for a rule's owner. Here they are made by code from the rule as the problem holds it. In your own work they would be the examples you keep for a rule, and the check is only as good as they are.
  • The attack is evidence, not a proof. Many forged replies found no leak, and a second reader agreed every time. That does not show no forged reply exists.
  • The seal was made twice. After the first seal, the sealed run was begun, and the first check on GitHub found that the solver's attack made different claims under different JavaScript engines (a sort that drew at random). The attack was fixed, the build sealed again and the sealed run started again from nothing; nothing the model is shown or judged by changed, and the 34 replies made under the first seal are kept. SEAL.md tells it in full.
  • The check holds the question. It knows which figures a question needs and how they combine. The model does the reading and every step of the sum. For your own work, that bookkeeping is the part you would write.
  • The model did not get smarter, and nothing here retrains it. It is the same small model at the last facet as at the first.
How it is built

"Gated 41/41" means a mutation test changed that part's code 41 ways, one small change at a time (a < for a <=, a + for a -), and its tests caught every one. "Equivalent" is a change that makes no difference to what the code does, so no test can catch it; each has its reason written down in src/witness.baseline.json.

For your business

What it is. A way to get answers out of your own documents and rules from a small model you run yourself, where every figure is shown in the document it came from, every formula is run on its rule's tests, every claim is decided by a solver, every sum is exact, and every answer says how far it can be trusted. In the published sealed run, 139 of 144 problems came out as they should; 0 answers that stand on cited figures and exact steps were other than what their document gives.

Where it fits, and where it does not

It fits questions whose answer is figures from a document, a rule applied to them, and arithmetic: what an order comes to under a contract, what is owed after a deposit, what a term costs in total, and whether a range of orders is certain to clear a limit. It does not fit judgement, summaries, or anything a program cannot check. Where it cannot check, it gives no answer.

For each role
  • Chief executive. No answer leaves without saying what it stands on. A figure from a contract is shown in the contract. A document that contradicts itself gets no answer, and the page says which figure and why.
  • Finance. The cost is small model calls on hardware you own. 4.1 model calls per problem in the published sealed run, each a short request to the small model on this device: copy one line, write one rule or claim, or work out one step. There is no charge per question.
  • Operations. When it stops, it names the cell it could not bond and the reason (the figure it could not cite, the test the formula failed, the claim it could not decide), so a person picks up from there, not from the beginning.
  • Technology and security. One page, no server, no account, no key. The document, the model and all four checks stay in the browser. The model's files come from the public WebLLM library, and this page does not check them against a fixed fingerprint (a hash), so a changed file upstream would not be caught here: a limit stated plainly.
  • Legal and compliance. Every cited figure carries the document's hash and the character offsets of the quote, so anyone can open the document and see it. Nothing is paraphrased: a quote is the document's own words or it is refused.
Against what you use now

In the published sealed run, the same model in one unchecked reply got 34 of 144 problems right, and on the 24 with no answer it gave a number 22 times. With the checks, 139 of 144 came out as they should, and none of those 24 was answered. A general assistant will answer from a document whether or not the figure is there. This does not: where the document is silent or says two things, it gives no answer.

Rolling it out
  1. Pick one kind of question whose answer is figures from a document and a sum.
  2. Write down, for that question, which figures it needs and how they combine.
  3. Run your own documents and count: answered, refused, and why.
  4. Read the refusals first. Each one names a clause to fix or a question to reword.
  5. Keep the label on every answer you pass on: grounded means "as true as the document".
Security, data and ownership

A document you paste is handled in the tab; the page makes no request while it works, and the count is measured by the browser. Risks, answered straight
  • "What if the document is wrong?" Then the answer is wrong, and labelled grounded. Nothing here can check a document against the world.
  • "It refuses too much." It refuses when the figure is absent, stated twice, not written plainly, or asked for in other words. In the published sealed run: 25 of 280 figures asked for did not pass the citation check, 24 of them because the document could not give them.
  • "It only does figures, rules, simple claims and sums." Yes, here. All four checks are built, each for a narrow kind of work. Where none of them can check, nothing bonds.
  • "Can a small model write a rule, or a claim?" It is measured. In the published sealed run its formula passed the rule's tests for 24 of 24 rules, and its claim gave the question's own verdict for 24 of 24 claims.
  • "A small model cannot read." It is measured. In the published sealed run its quote bonded for 255 of 256 figures that were there.

White paper: one label per kind of truth

The problem. A model that answers questions about a document can quote a figure that is not there, work on a figure it misread, write a rule down wrongly, or answer a yes-or-no question it never worked out. On the page all four look the same: a confident answer. Checking one kind of work, the arithmetic say, leaves the others unchecked, and an answer that rests on an unchecked part is still reported as if all of it were checked.

The idea. Give each kind of work its own check, and give what each check proves its own name. Exact arithmetic is proven. A figure found in the document, word for word, is grounded: as true as the document. A formula that passed its rule's tests is proven against its tests. A claim a solver decided is proven within its encoding: exact about the claim as it was written. Then label every answer by the weakest check beneath it, so no answer is called truer than what it stands on. Where no check can say yes, give no answer, and say which piece failed and why.

How a check is trusted. A check is only as good as the attack it survives. Each one here comes with a harness that forges replies many ways, a second reader written apart that must rule the same way on every reply, and twelve copies of the check with one leak planted in each, which the harness must find. Every check and every harness is mutation-gated: its tests must catch every small change to its code.

What this page tests. Whether a small model in a browser tab, held to these four checks, brings more problems out as they should come out than the same model answering once with nothing checking it; whether any checked answer is wrong; and whether any is labelled stronger than it stands on. The problems were sealed before the run and twelve claims with their bars were written down first; the numbers above show each one's result.

What it does not show. That a grounded answer is true: it is as true as its document. That a formula that passes its tests is right: one built to pass exactly those tests passes too, and the page shows it. That a decided claim is the claim that was meant: the solver is exact about what the model wrote. That the model got better: nothing here trains it.

The one rule. No answer is called truer than its weakest check, and every number on this page comes from a recorded run that the page can grow again, without the model, to the same result.
